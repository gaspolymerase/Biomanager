User guide

Using BioManager

Everything BioManager does, and how to move a lab into it — from a first launch to a whole lab on a shared server. New here, or coming from spreadsheets? Read Coming from Excel first: it takes ten minutes and saves you days.

Part one

Start here

What BioManager is

BioManager is one place for what a biology lab keeps: animals (mice, zebrafish, flies, worms, or any organism you describe), plasmids, samples, orders, reagents and antibodies, with a calendar and a notebook beside them. It looks and edits like a spreadsheet, but underneath it is a database that understands your lab: it knows a mouse lives in a cage on a rack, that a litter born today is weaned at P21, that a vial at 18 °C is flipped half as often as at 25 °C, and that an antibody expires next month.

It comes in two forms, the same program in both:

Desktop appFor one person. macOS, Windows or Linux; your data stays on your computer.
Lab serverFor a whole lab: one BioManager that everyone opens in a browser, the Android app, or from an iPhone's home screen. Usually free to host. Every way to run one.

Coming from Excel

If your lab runs on spreadsheets, you already know most of BioManager. The sheet is still there — click a cell and type — but each thing Excel makes you do by hand, BioManager does for you.

Move in, in about half an hour

  1. Save your sheets as CSV. In Excel, File → Save As → CSV UTF-8, one file per sheet.
  2. Mice. Rename your headers to ones BioManager recognises — sex, dob, cage, litter, notes, and if you have them transgene_1…transgene_4, genotype, owner, status, cage_location. Other columns are ignored. Leave out your old IDs: BioManager numbers mice in order. Then Mice → Add many → Or load a CSV → Preview file, check the preview, fix anything in place, and Create.
  3. Racks. Cages → Rack grid → New rack, labelled like your real stickers, then drag each cage from Unplaced onto its position.
  4. Orders. Orders → Import with the headers item_name, vendor_name, catalog_number, quantity, status, requester_name, notes. A preview shows any row it can't read before anything is saved.
  5. Plasmids. Plasmids → Import with name, backbone, insert_seq, resistance, owner, location, notes.
  6. Everything else: paste a column. In any inventory, and in Plasmids, Add many takes a list pasted straight from Excel — one name per line becomes one record each. Fill in the shared details once.
  7. Print cage cards and put them on the cages. From now on, the spreadsheet is the backup, not the record.

What you get that a spreadsheet can't do

It tells you what to do today

Weanings due, pups to genotype, breeders past 30 weeks, vials to flip, reagents expiring — gathered on Home each morning.

You see the room

Racks, incubators and freezer boxes drawn as grids with every position labelled your way. Drag to move.

Nothing is lost

Every edit is kept with who and when; any bulk change can be undone; the server backs itself up every night and proves the backup restores.

Scan instead of typing

Point a phone at a cage card and that cage opens, ready to edit, at the rack.

Yours and the lab's

You edit what you own; the lab's common stock is everyone's; admins see it all. No more "who changed my column?".

It fits your organism

Describe a new species — its words, how it's counted, what it needs — and BioManager builds its database. No code.

Install and set up

Get it

macOSDownload, unzip, drag BioManager into Applications. The first time, right-click it → Open, then Open.
WindowsExtract the zip, open the BioManager folder, run BioManager.exe. At the warning choose More info → Run anyway.
LinuxMark BioManager-Linux.AppImage executable (right-click → Properties, or chmod +x) and open it.
A lab serverNothing to install: open the address your admin gives you. Setting one up is on Run it for your lab.
AndroidInstall BioManager-Android.apk from the download page and type your lab server's address once. See Phones.
iPhone, iPadOpen your lab server in Safari and tap Share → Add to Home Screen. It gets an icon and opens full screen, like an app.
Why the warning on a computer? The apps aren't signed by Apple or Microsoft, so the system asks you to confirm once per version.

The set-up survey

The first account is the lab's admin (on a server, it needs the one-time setup code printed when the server started). The admin then answers a short survey, and BioManager builds exactly what you'll use — nothing more:

  1. Your lab — its name.
  2. What does your lab keep? Tick animals and stocks (mice, zebrafish, flies, worms, other organisms) and molecular and supplies (plasmids, samples, orders, reagents, antibodies). For each, a little more: a name (e.g. Mouse room B); for the colony how many racks, their size and how positions are labelled; for flies and worms which incubator temperatures you run. Blank means the usual.
  3. Which lab functions? The calendar and the notebook.
  4. What may members do? Whether members may create databases of their own, and whether they may add databases for the whole lab.

BioManager then makes those databases, the racks and incubators too, and takes you Home. Everyone who joins later gets a one-page welcome tour of the lab's databases on first sign-in (Settings → See the welcome tour again brings it back).

Home

Home is where each day starts: only what needs attention, from every database you can see. Choose how it looks with the Layout chips at the top (or in Settings → Home layout); the choice is yours alone.

Classic
Cards: your databases as tiles, a few numbers (total mice, sac candidates, weanings, pending orders, notebook pages) and cards for Mice older than 30 weeks, Upcoming weanings, the Genotyping queue, Flies & worms: due, Expiring & low stock, zebrafish mating tanks to return, the Next 14 days and Recent orders.
Tracks
Every dated job on one ruler of days, one track per kind of work, coloured overdue, today, within 3 days or scheduled. Choose 1, 2 or 4 weeks. Needs you now and Busy days sit beside it. On a phone it becomes a list of days.
Freezer
Your racks seen from above, lit where something is due, with a Pull list of the next three days' jobs in walking order, to take into the room. A cage's cap colour shows its owner.

For you shows your unread notifications. While you're new, Getting started lists the first steps for what your lab keeps — add a rack, bring in your mice, print cage cards, set up your fly database, add to orders, invite the lab — and ticks each off when it's done. Hide this list puts it away.

Part two

Everyday skills

Every database works the same way. Learn it once here.

Working in a sheet

Edit in place
Click a cell and type, or pick from its list. It saves as you go; a note at the bottom says All changes saved. If a change can't be saved (say, a required field emptied) the cell turns red and says why.
The dot
A green dot beside the number means alive or usable: a living mouse, a vial in use, an open order, a reagent in stock.
Open a record
The pencil opens the full record in a dialog, with every field, grouped. New records open the same dialog.
Sort, Columns, Export, Print
Sort by any column, or click a heading. Columns hides what you don't need; drag an edge to resize. Export downloads the rows you see as CSV (mice: also Excel and PDF). Print gives a clean sheet. Your filters, hidden columns and widths are remembered on that computer.
Layouts
Where it fits, a sheet also shows as a Rack grid or Box grid (positions, drag to move) or a Board (a column per status; drag a card along).
Suggestions
Text columns suggest what's been typed before, so the lab spells a vendor or a unit one way.

Many at once

Add many
Describe one record and say how many — or, for mice, how many females and males. For mice you can load a CSV instead; in inventories and plasmids, paste a list of names, one per line. You always see an editable preview first, with the numbers that will be assigned: Fill down copies a value into the empty cells below, and nothing is saved until you press Create.
Import
Orders and plasmids take a CSV through Import, with a dry run that lists any row it can't read.
Select and act
Tick rows (shift-click selects a range) and a bar rises from the bottom: set a field, move, change owner or status, sac, retire, delete. Records you may not change are skipped and counted, never silently changed.
Duplicate
Copies a record as a new one of yours — the next aliquot, or the same order again.

Finding things

  • The search box above a sheet filters it as you type: an ID, a genotype, a cage, a note.
  • Chips beside it narrow it: All, Alive, Mine, Lab common, a status, Expiring soon.
  • Search everything with ⌘/Ctrl + K, from anywhere: mice, cages, litters, experiments, strains, vials, tanks, lines, clutches, plasmids, orders, samples, reagents, antibodies and notebook pages. Arrows to move, Enter to open.
  • Tabs. Every page you open becomes a tab along the top: drag to reorder, + for a new one, Alt + 1…9 to jump. They're still there tomorrow.

Undo and history

Batches (sidebar) lists every bulk change — an Add many, a move, a status change on fifty rows, an import — with Undo. If someone has edited those records since, BioManager says so and offers Undo anyway rather than silently overwriting their work. Everyone sees their own batches; admins see the lab's.

The Audit log (admins) records every create, edit and delete: when, who, which record, and exactly what changed — genotype: DBH-Cre → ∅.

Notifications

The bell at the top shows what happened while you were away: someone moved your animals or vials, picked from your breeders, recorded a genotype on your mice, an order you placed was ordered, arrived or was cancelled, and lab news such as being made admin. A daily note reminds you of pups waiting to be genotyped. Choose which kinds you get in Settings → Notifications; you're never notified of your own changes.

Part three

Your databases

Mouse colony

Tabs across the top: Mice, Cages, Litters, Breeders, Experiments, Strains and Presets. The My colony / Shared / Everyone switch changes what you see, never what you may edit.

Racks and cages

Cages → Rack grid → New rack: rows, columns and how positions are labelled — D7, 4-7, 7D or 1 to 80, like the stickers on your real racks. Cages without a position wait in Unplaced; drag them on. Each cage has a purpose (breeding, stock, experiments), an owner, a room and its mice. Select cages to set purpose or owner, move them, or Retire them.

Mice

  • New mouse for one, Add many for a group or a CSV (headers). Type new as the cage to put a whole group in one new cage.
  • Each mouse has sex, date of birth (age shown in weeks), status — breeder, experiment, geno, transfer, sac — up to four transgenes, a genotype, cage, litter, owner and notes.
  • Select mice to set a field (owner, status, transgenes, cage, note, date of death), Add to experiment, or Sac. Setting sac or a date of death ends the record; it stays in the history.

Breeding: from litter to weaning

  1. On a breeding cage, Litter born today records the birth. BioManager works out weaning (P21) and genotyping, which appear on Home and the calendar when due.
  2. Genotyping on the cage creates the litter with its father, mother and pup count; pups set to geno wait in the genotyping queue on Home until their results are entered.
  3. Wean opens Wean and distribute pups: add a row per new cage and say who goes where, or Just wean and distribute them later.

Breeders lists breeding cages with their productivity and flags breeders past 30 weeks. Pick takes a breeder as yours; its owner is notified.

Experiments, strains, presets

  • Experiments group mice into a cohort with a treatment plan and groups; add members one by one or + All mice in cage, and record body weights, with the trend worked out for you.
  • Strains is the lab's reference list of lines and alleles.
  • Presets (admins) keep the drop-down values for each column, so the lab uses the same words.

Zebrafish

Tanks
New tank or Add many (consecutive IDs), on a table or a rack grid. Purposes: stock, breeding, mating, experiment, quarantine. Select tanks to set purpose or owner, move, or mark genotyping. The tank view also has the Sacrifice log.
Fish
A group with a headcount, or individuals when you need them; statuses alive, transfer, geno, sac. Move or sac a selection.
Clutches
Set up a mating: the male and female tanks, how many of each, and when they go back. Mating tanks due back show on Home until you mark them Returned. New clutch records each fertilisation.
Lines
Your line catalogue with ZFIN names and founders.
Water
Add system for each recirculating system, and Log reading for temperature, pH, conductivity and salinity, with a chart. A reading can raise an alarm.

Flies and worms

Drosophila keeps vials in racks inside incubators; C. elegans keeps plates in boxes. Tabs: the vials or plates, Schedule, Genotypes, Incubators & racks, Frozen (worms) and Settings.

  1. Under Incubators & racks, add each incubator with its temperature, then racks or boxes inside it.
  2. New vial (or plate): genotype, purpose and position. Fly purposes: stock, experiment, cross, virgins, progeny, expansion, backup. Worm purposes: maintenance, experiment, cross, progeny, synchronized, RNAi, males, starved.
  3. Schedule shows what's overdue, today and coming up in the next 7, 14 or 30 days. Mark vials Flipped (worms: Chunked), Collect eggs into a new vial (worms: Pick progeny), and record scoring. Intervals follow the temperature: set them under Settings → Temperatures and timing, and a rack moved from 25 °C to 18 °C changes its schedule by itself.
  4. Worms: New frozen lot records a freeze; thaw one onto a new plate when you need it.

Discard retires a vial (it can be restored); Copy to a new vial starts the next generation. Settings also renames the words and purposes to your lab's.

Any other organism

Add database in the sidebar builds a database for anything else — Xenopus, axolotls, cell lines, yeast. Start from a mouse-like, zebrafish-like or blank organism and describe it:

  • Name and vocabulary — what to call an animal, its housing (tank, cage, plate), a line, a birth cohort and a breeding event, and whether age is shown in days, weeks, days post-fertilisation or generations. The whole interface then speaks your words.
  • How animals are counted — individuals, groups with a headcount, or groups with individuals when needed.
  • What it does — pick capabilities: housing, rack positions, a line registry, crosses, cohorts, rearing stages, pedigree, genotyping, derived schedules, environment logs, health observations, body weights, a cull log, cryo inventory, protocol and census, cost attribution, sample harvests.
  • Your own columns — text, long text, a genotype code, number, date, a choice, yes/no, a lab member, a link to a line, or a web link.
  • Who it is for — Just me (a personal database only you and admins see) or The whole lab.

Change any of it later under the database's Configure, without losing records. The same page offers inventories (samples, orders, reagents, antibodies, a custom list) and fly or worm stock databases.

Plasmids

  • Show them as a table, by storage box, or as a list; chips for Mine, Has sequence, In a box.
  • New plasmid takes a name, backbone, insert, resistance, owner and a box position (blank takes the next free one) — or paste a list of names to make several. Drop in a SnapGene .dna, GenBank, FASTA or plain-text sequence.
  • Open a plasmid to see its sequence and features on an interactive map you can edit (edits save themselves), full screen if you like; Storage and Properties are the other tabs.
  • Select plasmids to set owner or resistance, move them to a box, or delete. Import takes a CSV.

Samples, orders, reagents, antibodies

One engine, five starting points, each with its own columns and statuses:

SamplesTissue and material, linked to the animal it came from, stored at RT, 4 °C, −20 °C, −80 °C or LN₂ in a freezer box position. Available, in use, used up, discarded.
OrdersWhat the lab has asked for: vendor, catalogue number, quantity, price, grant account. Requested, ordered, received, cancelled — on a table or a Board you drag cards across.
ReagentsQuantity, concentration, CAS number, storage temperature, hazard, lot and expiry. In stock, low, empty, discarded.
AntibodiesHost, clonality, clone, conjugate, reactivity, applications, dilution, isotype, RRID, and where each vial is.
Custom listWhatever you define.

Ordering, start to shelf

  1. New order. Item, vendor, catalogue number and quantity are required (starred), so no order goes out half-filled. Type an item or catalogue number the lab has used before and pick it: the vendor, price and grant fill in from last time.
  2. Follow it. The status chips — Requested, Ordered, Received, Cancelled — show each stage in one tap; the person who requested it is notified as it moves.
  3. It arrives. Mark it received and BioManager asks Add it to stock?: a new reagent or antibody with its name, vendor, catalogue number, lot, quantity and expiry already filled in, opened for you to add where it's kept. (Later, the To stock button on the order does the same.)
  4. Running low? On any reagent or antibody, the cart button, Order again, opens a new order with its details and the quantity, price and grant of the last time it was ordered.

Every inventory

  • Mine and Lab common keep your own stock apart from the lab's shared shelf.
  • Expiring soon (30 days) and Expired chips; expired and low stock also show on Home.
  • Box grid places items in freezer boxes; the same boxes as everywhere else.
  • Configure (its creator, or an admin): the name, what it tracks, the choices and statuses (renaming one relabels its items), your own columns, and which fields are needed for a new item.

Calendar

  • Month, Week, Day and List views, with colony dates — weanings, genotyping, sac reminders — filled in for you.
  • New event offers an event, a to-do (due today, tomorrow, next week, or a date), Book equipment (bookings of one instrument can't overlap), Time away, and Start a protocol from a saved multi-step protocol.
  • Connected calendars shows Google Calendar and any iCal subscription alongside, read-only.
  • On your phone: make a private feed link (just yours, or the whole lab's) and subscribe to it in your phone's calendar.

Notebook and utilities

  • Pages in Topics, with Recents and a filter. Headings, lists, task lists, quotes, tables, images and attached files.
  • Templates for pages you write often: save any page as one, and start new pages from it.
  • Insert… adds live tables — a list of mice, plasmids or inventory items, a results table, a mixture-prep table, a saved search — and code blocks and symbols.
  • Type @ to link a mouse, plasmid or order; the page and the record then point at each other.
  • Utilities (sidebar): mass from concentration (molecular weight, target mM, final mL) and a chemical reference list.

Part four

Working as a lab

On a lab server, everyone works in the same live database.

Who can do what

  • You edit what you own — your mice, cages, vials, plasmids and items.
  • Breeding cages and anything marked shared or lab common belong to the whole lab.
  • Everyone sees the lab's databases, because a census with holes is not a census.
  • Personal databases (Just you on their tile) are seen only by their owner and admins.
  • Admins can change anything, run Lab setup, approve people, and read the audit log.
  • Racks & boxes (admins) says who looks after each rack, box and incubator.

When a record isn't yours, BioManager says whose it is instead of just refusing.

People and guests

Joining
New sign-ups wait for an admin's approval in Manage users. Members can also sign in with Google or Microsoft if the server offers it, and connect it under Settings → Sign-in methods.
Admins
Manage users promotes and demotes admins, disables accounts and resets passwords; Lab setup can also make someone admin. Keep at least two.
Leaving
Overview lists every cage by who manages it, idle cages and living mice without a cage; hand racks over in Racks & boxes, then disable the account. Their records and history stay.
Guests
For someone outside the lab — a collaborator, a visitor — for a day to a month: Guests in the account menu, who it's for and how long, Make a code. They open the link, enter the code, and work as a member with their own account until the pass ends or you press End now. On a Tailscale server, the admin can let guests in from the internet without Tailscale (how).

Lab setup

Setup in the sidebar (admins) reopens the survey at any time: switch databases on or off (switching off hides a database and keeps its data), rename the lab, choose the calendar and notebook, and set what members may do — create their own databases, or add ones for the whole lab. Under Who looks after the lab, make someone admin.

Phones and cage cards

Android
Install BioManager-Android.apk from the download page (allow your browser to install apps when asked), type your lab server's address once, and you're in. The Scan button reads cage cards.
iPhone and iPad
Open the server in Safari and tap Share → Add to Home Screen. BioManager gets its own icon and opens full screen like an app; your camera scans cage cards. (Android without the app: Chrome → ⋮ → Install app.)
Cage cards
Cages → Cage cards prints cards sized for standard card stock — owner, purpose, room, animals, genotype and a QR code. Scanning it opens that cage, ready to edit, at the rack.
Phones reach a server, not the desktop app: the app on one computer is private to that computer.

Part five

Data and help

Your data and backups

The desktop app

macOS~/Library/Application Support/Biomanager/
Windows%APPDATA%\Biomanager\
Linux~/.local/share/Biomanager/
  • Your data lives outside the app, so updating or reinstalling never touches it.
  • Back that folder up with Time Machine or File History, copying it while BioManager is closed.
Don't keep BioManager's data in Dropbox, OneDrive, Google Drive or iCloud Drive. Syncing corrupts database files. BioManager warns you if it finds itself in one.

A lab server

A server backs up every night, restores the newest backup every week to prove it works, and can keep encrypted copies off-site — see Keeping the data safe.

A copy of the lab on your computer

The desktop app can keep a copy of your lab server's whole database, so the lab's records live on more than one machine:

  1. On the server: Settings → Copies of the lab on your computers, name the computer and Make a key. Copy the key; it's shown once. (Admins always may; members when Lab setup allows it.)
  2. On that computer, in the desktop app: Settings → Keep a copy of your lab server. Enter the server's address and the key, and Save and copy now.
  3. From then on, while the app is open, it takes a fresh copy every day, checks it, keeps the last 14 and mirrors uploaded files. The card shows when the last copy was made; on the server, the list shows every computer and how fresh its copy is. Revoke stops a computer (a lost laptop, say) from taking more.

The copies sit beside the app's own data, which they never touch. A copy can rebuild the server if it's ever lost (how). Treat a computer holding one like a lab notebook: it has every record.

Everyone

Export on any sheet downloads what it shows as CSV, which opens in Excel. Settings → Export my data downloads your own mice and plasmids as CSV and your notebook pages, in one zip.

Troubleshooting

The app won't open on my Mac
Right-click it and choose Open. If macOS says it's damaged, move it to Applications first, then try again.
Linux: the AppImage does nothing
Mark it executable. If it still won't start, install libfuse2, or use the .tar.gz and run BioManager/BioManager.
My phone can't reach the server
Check the address with your admin; that you're on the lab's network, VPN or Tailscale; and, if your lab uses its own certificate, that it's installed on the phone.
I can't edit a record
It belongs to someone else and isn't shared: BioManager says whose it is. Ask them, or an admin.
A cell turned red
That change wasn't saved; the message at the bottom says why (a required field, a date that isn't one).
I made a mistake with a bulk change
Batches in the sidebar → Undo.
A database disappeared from the sidebar
An admin may have switched it off in Setup; its data is kept.
Something else
Open an issue — say which system you use and what you expected to happen.
